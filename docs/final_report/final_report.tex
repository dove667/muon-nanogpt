\section{Problem Setup}
\textbf{Problem statement}
Muon is a recently proposed optimizer that updates matrix parameters by orthogonalizing a momentum-like update direction through a short Newton--Schulz (NS) iteration \citep{jordan2024muon,moddednanogpt2024}. Recent theory has also started to analyze the convergence properties of Muon-style NS updates \citep{kim2026convergence}. In practice, the iteration is controlled by quintic coefficients, so schedule design becomes a spectral design problem rather than a minor implementation detail. This project asks:
\emph{under a fixed transformer pretraining stack, how do different NS coefficient schedules change the spectral geometry of Muon updates, and how does this spectral geometric change affect validation loss and training cost?}

\textbf{Mathematical formulation}
To understand Muon, it is helpful to start from the limitation of AdamW. For a weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, AdamW treats the gradient elementwise. This is effective, but it ignores the fact that $W$ is a matrix with coupled row and column geometry. In particular, an update can have very uneven singular values, meaning that some directions are stretched much more strongly than others. Muon is motivated by the idea that for hidden-layer matrix parameters, a better update should acts more isotropically on the sigular directions and avoids concentrating into a few dominant directions.

For a rectangular matrix $X$,  $X$ is semi-orthogonal if:
\begin{equation}
    X^\top X \approx I_n \quad \text{if } m\ge n,
    \qquad
    X X^\top \approx I_m \quad \text{if } m<n.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, if $X=U\Sigma V^\top$ is an SVD, then the ideal semi-orthogonal factor is the polar factor $UV^\top$, whose nonzero singular values are all equal to $1$. Muon utilizes an iterative algorithm called NS iteration instead of SVD to approximately orthogonalize the update matrix before applying it to the parameters.

Why can NS iterations produce a semi-orthogonal matrix? The reason is that they act on singular values. After a normalization step, Muon repeatedly applies a quintic matrix polynomial. If $X_k$ is the matrix after the $k$-th NS iteration, then
\begin{equation}
    X_{k+1} = a_k X_k + b_k (X_k X_k^\top) X_k + c_k (X_k X_k^\top)^2 X_k,
\end{equation}
or the aspect-ratio-swapped equivalent when the matrix is tall. Because this map is equivariant to the SVD, it leaves singular vectors unchanged and transforms only the singular values. At the scalar level,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{k+1} = p_k(\sigma_k), \qquad
    p_k(\sigma)=a_k \sigma + b_k \sigma^3 + c_k \sigma^5.
\end{equation}
Therefore, a coefficient schedule $\{(a_k,b_k,c_k)\}_{k=1}^{T}$ defines a composed spectral map on singular values. If the composed map pushes the relevant singular-value range toward $1$, then the resulting matrix becomes closer to the polar factor and hence closer to semi-orthogonality. This is the mathematical core of Muon: orthogonalization is implemented indirectly by shaping the singular-value dynamics with a short polynomial iteration. The high level of a muon update procedure can be summarized as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:muon-high-level}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Muon update procedure}
\label{alg:muon-high-level}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialize momentum state $M_t$
\For{$t=1,2,\dots$}
    \State Compute matrix gradient $G_t=\nabla_W \mathcal{L}_t(W_{t-1})$
    \State Update accumulated momentum $M_t \leftarrow \mu M_{t-1} + G_t$
    \State Apply NS iteration on $M_t$ to obtain $O_t$
    \State Update parameters: $W_t \leftarrow W_{t-1} - \eta_t O_t$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This perspective also explains why coefficient choice matters. Different coefficients create different scalar maps $p_k$: some are conservative and stable, some are more aggressive and rapidly amplify small singular directions, and some are designed adaptively from a presumed lower bound on the spectrum \citep{amsel2025polar}. In other words, Muon is not just one optimizer; it is a family of optimizers parameterized by spectral maps. In this project, we systematically compare four coefficient schedules of Muon along with a AdamW baseline:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AdamW}: no NS orthogonalization on matrix parameters.
    \item \textbf{Stable}: conservative mapping coefficient $(2.0,-1.5,0.5)$.
    \item \textbf{Manual}: fast-to-stable schedules
    \item \textbf{Fast}: aggressive mapping coefficient $(3.4445,-4.7750,2.0315)$.
    \item \textbf{Polar Express (PE)}: dynamically compute coefficient at each step \citep{amsel2025polar}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Technical Approach}
\textbf{Experimental design}
The philosophy of the experiment is to isolate the effect of the NS coefficient schedule itself. For that reason, we use a controlled design in which the architecture, data source, token budget, learning-rate schedule, batch structure, and all Muon hyperparameters other than the orthogonalization schedule are held fixed across runs. Under this design, observed differences can be interpreted as consequences of spectral-map design rather than of unrelated tuning changes.

AdamW is included as an external baseline because it provides a useful reference point for what happens when matrix parameters are not orthogonalized. Within the Muon family, we fix the NS iteration depth to $T=5$ steps for the core schedule comparison. We further conduct several ablation studies on important Muon hyperparameters, especially the iteration depth and the PE lower-bound parameter. Finally, we combine the training results and mathematical analysis of the NS iteration to better interpret the relation between semi-orthogonal updates and validation loss in language pretraining.

\textbf{Evaluation dimensions}
The study evaluates each method along three dimensions. First, we measure \emph{optimization quality} through validation-loss trajectories, final validation loss, and token-normalized area under the validation-loss curve (AUC). Second, we measure \emph{computational cost} through end-to-end wall-clock time, because a schedule that improves geometry but is too expensive would be less practically useful. Third, we measure \emph{spectural geometry} directly by analyzing update matrices during training. This geometric analysis is essential: without it, any difference in final loss would remain a black-box empirical observation rather than a mathematically interpretable effect of the NS map.

\textbf{Experimental setup}
All experiments are run on one RTX 4090 GPU in BF16 precision using FineWeb-10B tokenized shards \citep{penedo2024fineweb}. The model is a standard 11-layer pre-norm Transformer with model dimension 768, 6 attention heads, head dimension 128, MLP ratio 4, and sequence length 2048. The total training budget is fixed at 100M tokens, with 131{,}072 tokens per optimizer step, 16 gradient-accumulation steps, 2\% warmup, and cosine decay to 20\% of the peak learning rate.

The optimizer settings are chosen to match a realistic small-transformer pretraining regime:

\begin{itemize}
    \item AdamW baseline: learning rate $6\times 10^{-4}$, $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.95)$, weight decay $0.1$.
    \item Muon family: base learning rate $0.023$, momentum $0.95$, $\beta_2=0.9$, weight decay $1.2$.
\end{itemize}

These values lie in the empirically established range commonly used in modern small-transformer pretraining practice and in public Muon baselines~\cite{moddednanogpt2024}, so they should be viewed as reasonable default settings rather than arbitrary picks.

\textbf{Muon variant used in this project}
The project does not use the simplest momentum SGD shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:muon-high-level}. The actual matrix update path is a \emph{Nesterov-style} lookahead variant together with second-moment rescaling on the , so more precisely it is a normalized Muon implementation. Let $G_t$ denote the matrix gradient at step $t$, and let $B_t$ be the momentum buffer. The update path is

\begin{align}
    B_t &= m B_{t-1} + (1-m) G_t, \\
    g_{\mathrm{pre},t} &= m B_t + (1-m) G_t, \\
    g_{\mathrm{post},t} &= \mathcal{P}(g_{\mathrm{pre},t}),
\end{align}

where $\mathcal{P}$ is the schedule-defined NS orthogonalization map. The use of $g_{\mathrm{pre},t}$ means the optimizer does not orthogonalize the raw buffer itself, but a Nesterov-style lookahead combination that effectively looks one step ahead along the momentum direction.

After orthogonalization, the implementation further applies a second-moment normalization on the matrix update magnitude before weight decay and the learning-rate step. In that sense, our project studies coefficient schedules inside the specific normalized Muon variant actually used by this training stack. This detail is important for interpreting the results, because the measured update geometry is the geometry of the real implemented search direction rather than of an idealized Muon formula.

For the spectral geometric study, every 10M training tokens we sample representative update matrices and record singular-value statistics together with a short-side semi-orthogonality error:
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(X)=
    \begin{cases}
        \lVert X^\top X-I\rVert_F, & X \text{ tall or square},\\
        \lVert X X^\top-I\rVert_F, & X \text{ wide}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This metric is applied to \texttt{buffer\_post}, \texttt{g\_pre}, and \texttt{g\_post}. It allows us to distinguish between geometry inherited from the training trajectory before orthogonalization and geometry created directly by the NS map after orthogonalization.

\subsection{Spectral Geometry Results}
\textbf{Newton--Schulz schedules create visibly different update geometries.} Table~\ref{tab:spectral} aggregates the sampled semi-orthogonality errors across training. The first observation is that the error drops from \texttt{g\_pre} to \texttt{g\_post} for every schedule, confirming that the NS map is actively doing the intended geometric work at every step. However, the drop varies enormously: the conservative Stable coefficients reduce the error only to $0.9006$, which is substantially higher than more aggressive schedules.

The second observation is that the schedules remain clearly separated after orthogonalization, and this separation is monotone in schedule aggressiveness. Stable ends at $0.9006$, Fast at $0.6444$, Manual $T=5$ at $0.6866$, Manual $T=9$ at $0.4431$, and PE $T=9$ at $0.3781$. This ordering is consistent with the polynomial amplification analysis in Section~\ref{sec:map}: deeper and more aggressive schedules push a larger fraction of the singular-value mass toward $1$, and the residual semi-orthogonality error reflects how far the actual update is from the ideal polar factor. Notably, deeper Manual and PE schedules achieve smaller \texttt{g\_post} error than Fast, even though their final validation losses are comparable or slightly higher---a finding that motivates the separate analysis in Section~\ref{sec:map}.

\textbf{Schedule sensitivity is larger in attention layers than in MLP layers.} Table~\ref{tab:module} and the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:spectral} break the \texttt{g\_post} error down by module type. The asymmetry is substantial. In attention layers, moving from Fast to PE $T=9$ reduces the error from $0.5146$ to $0.0481$---a reduction by a factor of about $10$. In MLP layers, the same schedule change reduces the error only from $0.7743$ to $0.7081$, a change of less than $10\%$.

This pattern suggests that attention projection matrices and MLP weight matrices experience different spectral regimes during training. MLP matrices appear to maintain a wider spread of singular values that is harder to fully orthogonalize even with deeper schedules, while attention projections respond more cleanly to increased schedule aggressiveness. One interpretation is that the effective rank of gradient directions is lower in attention layers, so a stronger polynomial can more efficiently collapse the singular-value distribution toward $1$. The result has a practical implication: if the goal is to tighten update geometry in a specific module, schedule depth matters most for attention layers, and adding NS steps beyond $T=5$ yields diminishing returns in MLP layers specifically.

\subsection{Spectral Map Analysis}
\label{sec:map}

The geometry differences observed above have a precise mathematical origin: different schedules define different composed scalar maps on singular values, and these maps determine how aggressively small singular directions are promoted toward the polar-factor target.

\textbf{Single-step behavior} For a schedule with $T$ NS steps, the composed scalar map $p_T$ is obtained by iterating $p_k(\sigma)=a_k\sigma+b_k\sigma^3+c_k\sigma^5$. Figure~\ref{fig:single-step} shows the single-step behavior. The Fast coefficient map amplifies small singular values much more strongly than the Stable NS map, but its derivative passes through zero and becomes negative in part of the input range. This means Fast behaves less like a conservative contraction and more like an aggressive spectral reshaping rule: it is not monotone in singular-value rank, and the mapping is order-reversing for certain input values. The key distinction between the Fast and Stable schedules is that instead of strictly attracting all singular values in $[0,1]$ toward $1$, the Fast map pushes them toward the range $[0.7, 1.3]$ and tolerates some oscillation around the target. The fact that Fast achieves the best training outcome suggests that validation loss is not monotone in the degree of semi-orthogonality: a moderate level of orthogonalization is beneficial, but maximally tight orthogonality is not necessary.

\textbf{Composed maps and amplification} After several steps, the local single-step differences accumulate into very different overall maps. Left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:maps} illustrates this: Stable barely lifts small singular values, Fast produces a noticeably steeper rise, and deeper Manual or PE schedules push small singular values much closer to $1$. The right panel illustrates the composed-map behavior of PE under different lower-bound choices. The convergence behavior depends strongly on the lower-bound assumption $\ell$: PE with $\ell=10^{-3}$ effectively attracts all singular values above $10^{-3}$ to near $1$, while the alternative with $\ell=3\times10^{-5}$ exhibits oscillation across a wider input range, failing to produce a clean fixed-point attraction.

The contrast is striking: Stable has an amplification ratio of only $32$, leaving a singular value of $10^{-5}$ still negligibly small at $3.2\times10^{-4}$. Fast raises the amplification ratio to $485$, but the output is still far from the polar target. Manual at $T=9$ reaches an amplification ratio of $4502$ and pushes the output to $4.5\times10^{-2}$, while PE at $T=9$ achieves an amplification ratio of $7.1\times10^4$, mapping $10^{-5}$ all the way to $0.713$---within striking distance of the ideal value of $1$. These numbers make the geometric improvement in Table~\ref{tab:spectral} interpretable: it is not that different schedules apply more iterations to the same map, but that they define fundamentally different maps, and a single application of a well-designed map can accomplish what many steps of a conservative map cannot.

\textbf{Spectral perturbation structure} Figure~\ref{fig:derivatives} plots the derivative $p_T'(\sigma)$ of each composed map. The most striking feature is that \emph{all} schedules saturate at the $\pm 20$ clip limit for $\sigma \lesssim 10^{-3}$: in the deep spectral tail every schedule is equally extreme, so that region alone cannot explain the performance differences observed between schedules.

The informative region is the intermediate singular-value range $\sigma \in [0.01,\, 0.1]$, where gradient mass in typical language-model training is likely to concentrate. Here the schedules diverge qualitatively. Stable shows a smooth, monotonically decreasing derivative with no sign changes, meaning it preserves the relative ordering of singular directions throughout. Fast, by contrast, exhibits large oscillations with sign changes across this range, indicating that it actively inverts the importance ranking of some singular directions during orthogonalization. The deeper Manual and PE schedules have relatively flat derivatives in the moderate-$\sigma$ range, consistent with their role as maps that collapse the dominant spectral mass toward $1$ cleanly. PE with $\ell=3\times10^{-5}$ is an exception: the oscillatory behavior shifts to smaller $\sigma$, suggesting its polynomial is being stretched over a spectral interval that covers directions likely dominated by noise.

These observations do not yield a simple causal explanation for the non-monotone relationship between orthogonality and training loss. Rather, they suggest that the schedules differ not only in \emph{how much} they reshape the spectrum but in \emph{which part} of the spectrum they perturb most and in what manner. Whether a given perturbation aids or hurts training ultimately depends on how the affected singular directions align with the loss landscape---a quantity not directly readable from the map structure.

Together, the composed-map analysis reveals that schedule choice shapes not only how aggressively the NS iteration pushes singular directions toward the polar target, but also which part of the gradient spectrum is most actively perturbed. Schedules that achieve better semi-orthogonality do so by applying stronger, qualitatively different maps---yet this does not translate monotonically into lower validation loss. The derivative structure suggests that each schedule perturbs a different spectral region in a different manner, but whether any particular perturbation is beneficial or harmful depends on how those singular directions align with the loss landscape, which the map alone cannot determine.

\section{Conclusion}
This project asked how different Newton--Schulz coefficient schedules change the spectral geometry of Muon updates, and how those geometric changes relate to transformer pretraining behavior under a fixed experimental stack. The main conclusion is that the schedule matters because it changes the geometry of the actual matrix update, not merely because it changes an implementation detail inside the optimizer. At fixed depth, fast Muon and well-chosen PE schedules are much stronger than the stable NS schedule. This indicates that aggressive small-singular-value amplification is useful in the early-scale pretraining regime studied here.

The spectral evidence gives a more detailed picture. Different schedules produce different composed singular-value maps, and those maps are reflected in the measured geometry of real update matrices. Deeper Manual and PE schedules substantially reduce post-orthogonalization error, especially in attention layers. However, this cleaner geometry does not translate monotonically into lower validation loss: in the representative multi-seed runs, Fast remains the strongest practical baseline at 100M tokens, while the more orthogonal $T=9$ schedules are close but not better.

Overall, the experiments suggest a balanced view of NS schedule design in Muon. Fast coefficients are highly effective for the present small-transformer pretraining setting, while Manual and PE schedules provide a more controllable way to reshape the spectrum and study the geometry of the update. The most interesting open question left by the experiments is not simply how to make the update more orthogonal, but how the composed singular-value map should be designed to perturb the \emph{right} spectral directions---those that are informative for the loss---rather than merely maximizing amplification across the board.
